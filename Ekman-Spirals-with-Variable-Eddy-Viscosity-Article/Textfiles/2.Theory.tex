\section{Theory}
\subsection{Problem formulation}
The Ekman equation is derived from the steady-state Navier--Stokes equations on a rotating frame with shear flow. Assuming laplace friction, incompressible flow, negligible vertical motion, gives the horizontal momentum equations as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
    -fv &= -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{d} x}
    + \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}\left(\nu \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{\mathrm{d} z}\right), \\
    fu &= -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{d} y}
    + \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}\left(\nu \frac{\mathrm{d} v}{\mathrm{d} z}\right),
    \label{eq:NavierStokes}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}
where $u$ and $v$ are the horizontal velocity components, $f$ is the Coriolis parameter, $\rho$ is the fluid density, and $\nu$ is the viscosity. Far away from the surface, shear flow and friction becomes negligible and the flow will approach geostrophic balance, $(u,v)\rightarrow (u_g,v_g)$, where the friction term in \autoref{eq:NavierStokes} can be neglected. In this article, a Coriolis parameter of \SI{1e-4}{\per\second} and a zonal geostrophic wind will be used. Subtracting the geostrophic balance from \autoref{eq:NavierStokes} gives
\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
    -f(v-v_g) &=
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}\left(\nu \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{\mathrm{d} z}\right), \\
    f(u-u_g) &=
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}\left(\nu \frac{\mathrm{d} v}{\mathrm{d} z}\right).
    \label{eq:subtractedNavierStokes}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}
This problem can be formulated with one equation by introducing a complex velocity as 
\begin{align}
    U \equiv u + iv,
    \label{eq:ComplexVelocity}
\end{align}
and a corresponding complex geostrophic velocity. Using \autoref{eq:subtractedNavierStokes} with the complex velocities $U$ and $U_g$ the coupled equations can be combined into the Ekman equation
\begin{empheq}{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}
    \left(
    \nu \frac{\mathrm{d} U}{\mathrm{d} z}
    \right)
    =
    if(U-U_g),
    \label{eq:EkmanEquation}
\end{empheq}
with the boundary conditions
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:BoundaryConditions}
\begin{align}
    U(0) &= 0,
    \label{eq:BoundaryCondition1}
    \\
    U(z\to\infty) &= U_g.
    \label{eq:BoundaryCondition2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The surface wind angle can be determined by taking the argument of the infinitesimal change of the wind at the ground,
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \arg\left(\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=0}\right).
\end{equation}
The Ekman transport is defined as the mass flux per unit width for the entire Ekman spiral, which can be defined as
\begin{equation}
    T = \int_0^\infty \left[U - U_g\right] \mathrm{d}z,
\end{equation}
since the transport represents the net movement of the entire fluid column offset from the geostrophic wind aloft. The angle of the Ekman transport can consequently be determined by taking the argument, $\theta_T=\arg(T)$. 

\subsection{The vertical momentum flux}
The Ekman equation can be reformulated by introducing a complex vertical momentum flux as
\begin{equation}
    \tau=\nu\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}z}.
    \label{eq:UtoTau}
\end{equation}
Using this definition, and differentiating \autoref{eq:EkmanEquation} gives
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d}z^2}=i\kappa \tau,
    \label{eq:MomentumFlux}
\end{empheq}
where 
\begin{equation}
    \kappa \equiv \frac{f}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
Here, an inverse decay scale $\kappa$ has been introduced to the system. \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is similar to equations such as the Helmholtz equation and the time-independent Schrödinger equation, where $\kappa$ becomes analogous to the potential. The new equation also has a clear physical interpretation, as it describes the vertical structure of the momentum flux in the Ekman problem. Using \autoref{eq:EkmanEquation} and \ref{eq:UtoTau}, the boundary conditions become
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions}
\begin{align}
    \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=0} &= -ifU_g,
    \label{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions1}
    \\
    \tau(z\to\infty) &= 0.
    \label{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Since the viscosity is always a positive real value the surface angle can also be expressed in terms of $\tau$ as
\begin{equation}
\theta = \arg\left[\tau(z=0)\right].
\end{equation}
Solving for the Ekman transport becomes easier by using the momentum flux, as it directly reduces the integral of the Ekman transport. Inserting \autoref{eq:EkmanEquation} into \ref{eq:UtoTau} it follows that $\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}=if\left(U-U_g\right)$. Using the boundary condition that the momentum flux vanishes at infinity the Ekman transport reduces to
\begin{equation}
T = \int_0^\infty \left[U - U_g\right] \mathrm{d}z = \frac{i}{f}\tau(z=0),
\label{eq:Transport}
\end{equation}
which is proportional to the vertical momentum flux at the surface. Thus, the surface stress at the ground is proportional to the Ekman transport of the fluid column.

\subsection{The classical solution}
The classical solution to the Ekman equation is obtained when the viscosity is assumed to be constant with altitude, $\nu=\text{constant}$, and was found by \citet{ekman1905}. In this case the classical Ekman spiral solution is
\begin{equation}
    \tau(z)=\nu U_g \frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek}} e^{- \frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek}} z},
    \label{eq:EkmanConstant}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    h_\text{Ek}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{f}}
    \label{eq:EkmanHeight}
\end{equation}
is the Ekman height scale. The surface angle of the classical Ekman theory gives \ang{45}, since the solution is equally real and imaginary at $z=0$. The transport yields an angle of \ang{90} from the surface wind-turing, meaning that the transport and surface wind is perpendicular.

\subsection{The two layer model}
The next natural step from a constant viscosity profile is to assume two layers of constant viscosity in each layer. The lower level is labelled as having the viscosity $\nu_1$ up until a height of $H$, where the next viscosity of $\nu_2$ takes over. From this assumption a superimposed solution similar to \autoref{eq:EkmanConstant} is obtained as 
\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
    \tau_1(z)&=A_1e^{\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}z}+B_1e^{-\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}z}, \\
    \tau_2(z)&=B_2e^{-\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek2}}z}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}
where the Ekman height scales are defined for each layer like in \autoref{eq:EkmanHeight}. To solve for the prefactors above one has to use that the velocity and momentum flux is continuous, which gives the jump conditions
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    U_1&=U_2 \quad \text{at } z=H, \\
    \tau_1&=\tau_2  \quad \text{at } z=H.
    \label{eq:JumpCondition}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Using the jump conditions together with the boundary conditions in \autoref{eq:BoundaryConditions} an expressions for the prefactors is obtained as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    A_1&=-\nu_1U_g\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}\cdot\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma+e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}\left(1+\gamma\right)}, \\ 
    B_1&=\nu_1U_g\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}\cdot\frac{\left(1+\gamma\right)e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}}{1-\gamma+e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}\left(1+\gamma\right)}, \\ 
    B_2&=\nu_2U_g\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek2}}\cdot\frac{2e^{\left(1+i\right)\varphi\left(1+\gamma^{-1}\right)}}{1-\gamma+e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}\left(1+\gamma\right)}, \\
    \ &\text{ where }\gamma=\sqrt{\tfrac{\nu_2}{\nu_1}}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and where $\varphi$ is the non-dimensional layer thickness defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \frac{H}{h_\text{Ek1}}.
\end{equation}

This provides a complete expression for the vertical momentum flux in the two-layer model, as shown in \autoref{fig:2layer_structure}. The figure illustrates how the vertical momentum flux varies with altitude for different viscosity ratios and non-dimensional heights, $\varphi$. For an increasing viscosity with height, a larger surface stress in the zonal direction compared to the meridional direction is observed. Conversely, for a smaller upper-layer viscosity, a larger meridional surface stress compared to the zonal surface stress is evident. Observing the interface, $H$, reveals that for larger $\varphi$, the model behaves more like a single-layer model, as the second layer begins at a height where the Ekman spiral has already decayed. Interestingly, smaller values of $\varphi$ yield a larger difference between the zonal and meridional wind stress.

By defining the constant
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}.
\end{equation}
and using the derived solution for the vertical momentum flux, the surface wind angle becomes 
\begin{align}
    \theta &= \arg\left[\left(A_1+B_1\right)\right] \\
    &=45\degr + 
    \arg\left[\frac{e^{(1+i)\varphi}-Re^{-(1+i)\varphi}}{e^{(1+i)\varphi}+Re^{-(1+i)\varphi}}\right],
    \label{eq:2layerAngle}
\end{align}
which can be observed as a function of $\varphi$ in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. The solutions for different viscosity ratios are shown, half of which have a higher upper-level viscosity and the other half have a lower upper-level viscosity. One can observe a convergence towards the classical Ekman value of \ang{45} when $\varphi\rightarrow\infty$ for all the viscosity ratios. This can be attributed to the high altitude interface $H$ being above the spiral, which reduces the problem to a one layer model. 

The zero limit is, however, dependent on the viscosity ratio, if this ratio is finite and fixed, the surface angle approaches \ang{45} as $H \rightarrow 0$. In this case the problem again reduces to a one-layer model. But if $\varphi$ is very small but fixed, the angle approaches a value close to 0 if $\nu_2 / \nu_1 \rightarrow \infty$, and a value close to \ang{90} if $\nu_2/\nu_1 \rightarrow 0$. In all these cases, $H$ is so small that  most of the Ekman spiral occurs above $z=H$. The solution of \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} in $0<z<H$ is then approximately a linear function, leading to a surface-dominated response where the surface stress aligns either perpendicular or parallel to the upper geostrophic flow. The lower layer works as a linear coupling between the surface and the upper layer, and the surface angle is thus determined by the momentum flux at $z=H$ together with the upper Ekman height.

In the case of weaker upper viscosity the perpendicular angle can be interpreted as the entire rotation, due to the Coriolis force, has to occur at the surface since the viscosity becomes negligible aloft. In the opposite case, a weaker viscosity at the ground makes the Ekman spiral less prominent. Since the Ekman spiral occurs due to the surface stress if no major viscosity is present the stress becomes negligible and the rotation does not occur. For $\nu_1>\nu_2$ (and $\nu_2>\nu_1$ respectively) the solution will always provide a result greater than \ang{45}, except between the $\varphi\in\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\pi\right)$ were a angle slightly below \ang{45} can be observed.

\subsection{The exponential decaying viscosity model}
Another viscosity profile that can provide a fully analytical solution is an exponentially decaying viscosity with altitude. Here the viscosity is set to decay from an initial viscosity of $\nu_0$, with a decay rate of $k$ as $\nu(z)=\nu_0\exp\left(-kz\right)$. Solving \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} with this viscosity profile can be done by introducing the new variable 
\begin{equation}
    t=2\left(1+i\right)\,\varphi_\text{exp}\, e^{\frac{kz}{2}}.
\end{equation}
The non-dimensional parameter $\varphi_\text{exp}$ resembles the non-dimensional layer thickness from the two-layer model, but defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_\text{exp}=\frac{1}{k\,h_\text{Ek0}}=\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{\frac{f}{2\nu_0}}.
\end{equation}
Using these newly defined variables \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} transforms into 
\begin{equation}
    t^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \tau}{\mathrm{d} t^2}+t\frac{\mathrm{d} \tau}{\mathrm{d} t}-t^2\tau=0,
\end{equation}
which is a differential equation with a standard solution. The standard solution is a modified Bessel equation as 
\begin{equation}
    \tau(t)=C_1I_0(t)+C_2K_0(t),
\end{equation}
where $C_1$ and $C_2$ are constants, while $I_0(t)$ and $K_0(t)$ are modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind of order zero, respectively. By applying the previously defined boundary conditions from \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions} the two constants can be found. Applying the geostrophic boundary condition from \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions2} shows that $C_1=0$ since $I_0(t)$ does not go to zero when $t\to\infty$ (which occurs when $z\to\infty$). Applying the surface boundary condition from \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions1} an expression for the second constant can be found. Transforming the solution to depend on the variable $z$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \tau(z)=fU_g\frac{1+i}
    {h_{\text{EK0}}}
    \frac{K_0\left(2\left[1+i\right]\varphi_\text{exp} e^{kz/2}\right)}{K_1\left(2\left[1+i\right]\varphi_\text{exp}\right)}.
    \label{eq:exponetial}
\end{equation}
This solution for the vertical momentum flux in the exponential model is shown in \autoref{fig:exponential_structure} for different non-dimensional heights, $\varphi_\text{exp}$. For smaller $\varphi_\text{exp}$, the solution is more localized toward the surface, whereas larger $\varphi_\text{exp}$ produces a more extended spiral. This implies that for larger $\varphi_\text{exp}$, the viscosity changes very little, causing the spiral to behave as in the one-layer model. In contrast, smaller $\varphi_\text{exp}$ indicates a rapid viscosity decrease from the ground.

    \label{fig:exponential_structure}
\end{figure*}

Using \autoref{eq:exponetial} the surface angle of this model is 
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \ang{45} + \arg\left[\frac{K_0\left(4\left[1+i\right]\,\varphi_\text{exp}\,\right)}{K_1\left(4\left[1+i\right]\,\varphi_\text{exp}\,\right)}\right],
    \label{eq:ExponetialAngle}
\end{equation} 
where $K_1$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order one. \autoref{eq:ExponetialAngle} is plotted in \autoref{fig:exponential_angle}. As this model only provides scenarios where the viscosity decreases from the ground, all angels are greater then the reference angle of \ang{45} (as predicted by the two layer model). One can observe a similar limit as in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle} when $\varphi_\text{exp}\rightarrow 0$ for $\nu_2=0$, as \autoref{eq:ExponetialAngle} also approaches \ang{90}. The same limit is also true for $\varphi_\text{exp}\rightarrow\infty$, which gives $\theta\rightarrow\ang{45}$, although this exponential solution converges slightly slower than the two-layer solution. The reasoning behind this convergence is that the lower decay rates makes the solution behave more like the classical Ekman spiral with constant viscosity. 

The opposite case when $\varphi_\text{exp}\ll1$ then $\tau(z)$ in \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} can be approximated as linear. Applying the lower boundary condition \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions1} gives 
\begin{equation}
    \tau(z)\approx \tau_0-ifU_gz.
\end{equation}
If the layer is very thin $\tau_0$ has to become completely imaginary in order to fulfil the vanishing upper boundary condition in \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions2}. Thus a perpendicular angle is obtained when $\varphi_\text{exp}\ll1$, which is the same limit as in the two-layer model.

\subsection{The simplified upper boundary condition model}
There is difficulty solving \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} due to the altitude dependence of the viscosity profile $\nu(z)$. Analytical solutions therefore exist only for specific viscosity profiles that allow the equation to be transformed into a solvable form. \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is furthermore also difficult to solve numerically, as the upper boundary condition is asymptotic. This will put limits on the viscosity profiles to study, as the viscosity cannot increase with altitude continuously. In the physical world, this makes sense, since we assume that high up in the atmosphere (or deep down in the ocean), the flow becomes laminar and the turbulent viscosity small. I.e. that the turbulent boundary layer has a boundary. However, by limiting ourselves to only decreasing viscosity profiles, the real-world differential equation becomes impossible to solve. 

A solution to solve this problem is to introduce a simplified upper boundary condition. For the two-layer model, this is reduced to a 1.5 layer model, where we assume that the upper-level viscosity is negligible ($\nu_2\approx0$), which corresponds to the upper dashed curve in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. This also makes physical sense, since we know that both the atmosphere and the ocean consist of a boundary layer where turbulent mixing takes place. Allowing the viscosity to vary freely in the lower layer reduces the problem to a simplified upper boundary condition model. This will allow viscosity profiles that do not converge at $z\rightarrow\infty$, since the new domain is between $z\in[0,H]$. This modifies the boundary conditions in \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions} as 
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:NewBoundaryConditions}
\begin{align}
    \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=0} &= -ifU_g,
    \label{eq:NewBoundaryCondition1}
    \\
    \tau(z=H) &= 0.
    \label{eq:NewBoundaryCondition2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $H$ is the height of the boundary layer, and the upper boundary condition was argued from the jump condition in the two-layer model, as seen in \autoref{eq:JumpCondition}.

\subsubsection{Simple viscosity profiles for the SUBC model}
Using the the simplified upper boundary condition, SUBC model, simple viscosity profiles can be studied and evaluated numerically. The easiest, except for the constant viscosity, is a linearly varying viscosity with height as 
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:LinearProfiles}   
 \begin{align}
    \label{eq:LinearIncreasing}
    \nu_\uparrow(z)&=\frac{\nu_\text{max}}{H}z, \\
    \label{eq:LinearDecreasing}
    \nu_\downarrow(z)&=\frac{\nu_\text{max}}{H}\left(H-z\right), 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\nu_\text{max}$ is the maximal viscosity. For both of these profiles a dimensionless vertical coordinate can be defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \tilde z = \frac{z}{H} \implies 
    \begin{cases}
        \nu_\uparrow(z)=\nu_\text{max}\tilde z \\
        \nu_\downarrow(z)=\nu_\text{max}\left(1-\tilde z\right). 
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Using these linear viscosity profiles, together with the new dimensionless vertical coordinate, \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is rewritten for the two profiles as 
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:SUBCLinear}
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:SUBCLinearIncreasing}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d} \tilde z^2}&=i\underbrace{\frac{1}{ \tilde z}}_{\equiv 1/\tilde\nu_\uparrow}\underbrace{\frac{H^2f}{\nu_\text{max}}}_{=2\varphi^2}\tau,\\
    \label{eq:SUBCLinearDecreasing}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d} \tilde z^2}&=i\underbrace{\frac{1}{ 1-\tilde z}}_{\equiv 1/\tilde\nu_\downarrow}\underbrace{\frac{H^2f}{\nu_\text{max}}}_{=2\varphi^2}\tau.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Above the dimensionless thickness $\varphi$ was again introduced following the logic from before.

Another simple profile to evaluate is a parabolic increasing viscosity as 
\begin{equation}
    \nu_p=\frac{4\nu_\text{max}}{H^2}\left(H-z\right)z.
    \label{eq:ParabolicProfile}
\end{equation} 
Again, using the non-dimensional height coordinate $\tilde z$ and the dimensionless thickness $\varphi$, \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d} \tilde z^2}=i \underbrace{\frac{1}{ 4\tilde z \left[1-\tilde z\right]}}_{\equiv 1/\tilde\nu_p} \underbrace{\frac{H^2f}{\nu_\text{max}}}_{=2\varphi^2}(U-U_g).
    \label{eq:SUBCParabolic}
\end{equation}
Since the height coordinate is normalized for the height $H$, the dimensionless thickness simply becomes the same as the inverse of the normalized Ekman scale height, defined as
\begin{equation}
    \varphi=\frac{H}{h_\text{Ek}}=\frac{1}{\tilde h_\text{Ek}}.
\end{equation}
