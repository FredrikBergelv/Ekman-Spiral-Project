\section{Theory}
\subsection{Problem formulation}
The Ekman equation is derived from the steady-state Navier--Stokes equations on a rotating frame with shear flow. Assuming laplace friction, incompressible flow, negligible vertical motion, gives the horizontal momentum equations as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
    -fv &= -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{d} x}
    + \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}\left(\nu \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{\mathrm{d} z}\right), \\
    fu &= -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{d} y}
    + \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}\left(\nu \frac{\mathrm{d} v}{\mathrm{d} z}\right),
\end{empheq}
\label{eq:NavierStokes}
\end{subequations}
where $u$ and $v$ are the horizontal velocity components, $f$ is the Coriolis parameter, $\rho$ is the fluid density, and $\nu$ is the viscosity. The flow approaches geostrophic balance $(u,v)\rightarrow (u_g,v_g)$ far away from the surface, where the friction term in \autoref{eq:NavierStokes} becomes negligible. This problem can be formulated with one equation by introducing a complex velocity as 
\begin{align}
    U \equiv u + iv,
    \label{eq:ComplexVelocity}
\end{align}
and a corresponding complex geostrophic velocity. Using \autoref{eq:NavierStokes} and \autoref{eq:ComplexVelocity} the coupled equations can be combined into the Ekman equation by subtracting the geostrophic balance as
\begin{empheq}{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} z}
    \left(
    \nu \frac{\mathrm{d} U}{\mathrm{d} z}
    \right)
    =
    if(U-U_g),
    \label{eq:EkmanEquation}
\end{empheq}
with the boundary conditions
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:BoundaryConditions}
\begin{align}
    U(0) &= 0,
    \label{eq:BoundaryCondition1}
    \\
    U(z\to\infty) &= U_g.
    \label{eq:BoundaryCondition2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The surface wind angle can be determined by the argument of the infinitesimal change of the wind at the ground,
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \arg\left(\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=0}\right).
\end{equation}
The Ekman transport is defined as the mass flux per unit width for the entire Ekman spiral, 
\begin{equation}
    T = \int_0^\infty \left[U - U_g\right] \mathrm{d}z,
\end{equation}
since the transport represents the net movement of the entire fluid column offset from the geostrophic wind aloft. The angle of the Ekman transport can consequently be determined by the argument, $\theta_T=\arg(T)$. 

\subsection{The vertical momentum flux}
The Ekman equation can be reformulated by introducing a complex vertical momentum flux as
\begin{equation}
    \tau=\nu\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}z}.
    \label{eq:UtoTau}
\end{equation}
Using this definition, differentiating \autoref{eq:EkmanEquation} gives
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d}z^2}=i\kappa^2 \tau,
    \label{eq:MomentumFlux}
\end{empheq}
where 
\begin{equation}
    \kappa^2 \equiv \frac{f}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
Here, an inverse decay scale $\kappa$ has been introduced to the system. \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is similar to equations such as the Helmholtz equation and the time-independent Schrödinger equation, where $\kappa$ becomes analogous to the potential. The new equation also has a clear physical interpretation, as it describes the vertical structure of the momentum flux in the Ekman problem. Using \autoref{eq:BoundaryConditions} and \ref{eq:UtoTau}, the boundary conditions become
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions}
\begin{align}
    \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=0} &= -ifU_g,
    \label{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions1}
    \\
    \tau(z\to\infty) &= 0.
    \label{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Since the viscosity is a real positive value the surface angle can be expressed with $\tau$ as
\begin{equation}
\theta = \arg\left[\tau(z=0)\right].
\end{equation}
The momentum flux directly reduces the integral of the Ekman transport. By inserting \autoref{eq:EkmanEquation} into \ref{eq:UtoTau} it follows that $\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}=if\left(U-U_g\right)$. Using the boundary condition that the momentum flux vanishes at infinity the Ekman transport reduces to
\begin{equation}
T = \int_0^\infty \left[U - U_g\right] \mathrm{d}z = \frac{i}{f}\tau(z=0),
\label{eq:Transport}
\end{equation}
which is proportional to the vertical momentum flux at the surface. Thus, the surface stress at the ground is proportional to the Ekman transport of the fluid column.

\subsection{The classical solution}
The classical solution to the Ekman equation is obtained when the viscosity is constant with altitude and was found by \citet{ekman1905}. In this case the solution is
\begin{equation}
    \tau(z)=\nu U_g \frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek}} e^{- \frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek}} z},
    \label{eq:EkmanConstant}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    h_\text{Ek}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{f}}
    \label{eq:EkmanHeight}
\end{equation}
is the Ekman height scale. The surface angle of the classical Ekman theory gives \ang{45}, since the solution is equally real and imaginary at $z=0$. The transport yields an angle of \ang{90} from the surface wind-turing, meaning that the transport and surface wind is perpendicular.

\subsection{The two layer model}
The next natural step from a constant viscosity profile is to assume two layers of constant viscosity in each layer. The lower level is labelled as having the viscosity $\nu_1$ up until a height of $H$, where the next viscosity of $\nu_2$ takes over. From this assumption a superimposed solution similar to \autoref{eq:EkmanConstant} is obtained as 
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:2layer_ansatz}
\begin{empheq}[left=\empheqlbrace]{align}
    \tau_1(z)&=A_1e^{\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}z}+B_1e^{-\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}z}, \\
    \tau_2(z)&=B_2e^{-\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek2}}z}
\end{empheq}
\end{subequations}
where the Ekman height scales are defined for each layer like in \autoref{eq:EkmanHeight}. The jump conditions are
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    U_1&=U_2 \quad \text{at } z=H, \\
    \tau_1&=\tau_2  \quad \text{at } z=H.
    \label{eq:JumpCondition}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Using the jump conditions together with the boundary conditions in \autoref{eq:BoundaryConditions} the prefactors become
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    A_1&=-\nu_1U_g\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}\cdot\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma+e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}\left(1+\gamma\right)}, \\ 
    B_1&=\nu_1U_g\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek1}}\cdot\frac{\left(1+\gamma\right)e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}}{1-\gamma+e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}\left(1+\gamma\right)}, \\ 
    B_2&=\nu_2U_g\frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek2}}\cdot\frac{2e^{\left(1+i\right)\varphi\left(1+\gamma^{-1}\right)}}{1-\gamma+e^{2\left(1+i\right)\varphi}\left(1+\gamma\right)}, \\
    \ &\text{ where }\gamma=\sqrt{\tfrac{\nu_2}{\nu_1}}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and where $\varphi$ is the non-dimensional layer thickness defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \frac{H}{h_\text{Ek1}}.
\end{equation}

This provides an solution for the vertical momentum flux in the two-layer model, as shown in \autoref{fig:2layer_structure}. The figure illustrates how the vertical momentum flux varies with altitude for different viscosity ratios and non-dimensional heights, $\varphi$. For an increasing viscosity with height, a larger surface stress in the zonal direction compared to the meridional direction is observed. Conversely, for a smaller upper-layer viscosity, a larger meridional surface stress compared to the zonal surface stress is evident. Observing the interface, $H$, reveals that for larger $\varphi$, the model behaves more like a single-layer model, as the second layer begins at a height where the Ekman spiral has already decayed. Interestingly, smaller values of $\varphi$ yield a larger difference between the zonal and meridional wind stress.

By defining the constant
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}.
\end{equation}
and using the derived solution for the vertical momentum flux, the surface wind angle becomes 
\begin{align}
    \theta &= \arg\left[\left(A_1+B_1\right)\right] \\
    &=45\degr + 
    \arg\left[\frac{e^{(1+i)\varphi}-Re^{-(1+i)\varphi}}{e^{(1+i)\varphi}+Re^{-(1+i)\varphi}}\right],
    \label{eq:2layerAngle}
\end{align}
which can be observed as a function of $\varphi$ in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. The solutions for different viscosity ratios are shown, half of which have a higher upper-level viscosity and the other half have a lower upper-level viscosity. The solution convergences towards the classical Ekman value of \ang{45} when $\varphi\gg 1$ for all the viscosity ratios. This can be attributed to the high altitude interface $H$ being above the spiral, which reduces the problem to a one layer model. 

The zero limit is, however, dependent on the viscosity ratio. If the ratio is finite and fixed, the surface angle approaches \ang{45} as $H \rightarrow 0$, again reducing the problem to a one-layer model. But if $\varphi$ is very small while the viscosity ratio varies, the angle approaches \ang{0} if $\nu_2 / \nu_1 \rightarrow \infty$, or an angle of \ang{90} if $\nu_2/\nu_1 \rightarrow 0$. The surface is thus sensitive to the viscosity ratio when $ \varphi $ is small, as the surface angle can be either above or below \ang{45} depending on the viscosity ratio. When $ \varphi \ll 1 $, the solution behaves approximately as a linear function between $0$ and $H$, allowing the jump conditions to determine the surface angle. \autoref{eq:2layer_ansatz} gives
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=H} \approx - \frac{1+i}{h_\text{Ek2}} \tau(H),
\end{equation}
which puts a constraint on $\tau(H)$ as $h_\text{Ek2}$ becomes very small or very large. If the viscosity ratio is very small, $\gamma\ll\varphi$, the momentum flux at the interface is very small, due to the small upper Ekman height $h_\text{Ek2}\ll H$ balancing a finite change in momentum flux. Taylor expanding the momentum flux at the interface gives 
\begin{equation}
    \tau(H)=\tau(0)+H\tau'(0)+\mathcal{O}\left(H^2\right).
\end{equation}
Using \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions1} and neglecting the small terms gives 
\begin{equation}
    \tau(0)\approx iU_gH \implies \arg[\tau(0)]\approx\ang{90}. 
\end{equation}
Similarly when $ \varphi \ll 1 $ and $\gamma\gg\varphi$, the change in the momentum flux has to become small, due to large upper Ekman height, $h_\text{Ek2}\gg H$ so that the change becomes small to balance the finite momentum flux. Integrating \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} gives 
\begin{equation}
    \tau'(H)-\tau'(0)= i\frac{fH}{\nu_1}\tau(0)+\mathcal{O}\left(H^2\right) \implies \tau(0)\approx \frac{\nu_1 U_g}{fH} \implies \arg[\tau(0)]\approx\ang{0}. 
\end{equation}
In the case of weaker upper viscosity the perpendicular angle can be interpreted as the entire rotation occurring at the surface since the viscosity becomes negligible aloft. In the opposite case, a weaker lower viscosity allowing minimal spiralling, since the absence stress cannot force a rotation. 
%For $\nu_1>\nu_2$ (and $\nu_2>\nu_1$ respectively) the solution will always provide a result greater than \ang{45}, except between the $\varphi\in\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\pi\right)$ were a angle slightly below \ang{45} can be observed.

\subsection{The exponential decaying viscosity model}
Another viscosity profile that can provide an analytical solution is an exponentially decaying viscosity with altitude. Here the viscosity is set to decay from an initial viscosity of $\nu_0$, with a decay rate of $k$ as $\nu(z)=\nu_0\exp\left(-kz\right)$. Solving \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} with this viscosity profile can be done by introducing the new variable 
\begin{equation}
    t=2\left(1+i\right)\,\varphi_\text{exp}\, e^{\frac{kz}{2}}.
\end{equation}
The non-dimensional parameter $\varphi_\text{exp}$ resembles the non-dimensional layer thickness from the two-layer model, but defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \varphi_\text{exp}=\frac{1}{k\,h_\text{Ek0}}=\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{\frac{f}{2\nu_0}}.
\end{equation}
Using these newly defined variables \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} transforms into 
\begin{equation}
    t^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \tau}{\mathrm{d} t^2}+t\frac{\mathrm{d} \tau}{\mathrm{d} t}-t^2\tau=0,
\end{equation}
which is a differential equation with a standard solution. The standard solution is a modified Bessel equation as 
\begin{equation}
    \tau(t)=C_1I_0(t)+C_2K_0(t),
\end{equation}
where $C_1$ and $C_2$ are constants, while $I_0(t)$ and $K_0(t)$ are modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind of order zero, respectively. By applying the previously defined boundary conditions from \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions} the two constants can be found. Applying the geostrophic boundary condition from \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions2} shows that $C_1=0$ since $I_0(t)$ does not go to zero when $t\to\infty$ (which occurs when $z\to\infty$). Applying the surface boundary condition from \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions1} an expression for the second constant can be found. Transforming the solution to depend on the variable $z$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \tau(z)=fU_gh_\text{Ek0}\frac{1+i}
    {2}
    \frac{K_0\left(2\left[1+i\right]\varphi_\text{exp} e^{kz/2}\right)}{K_1\left(2\left[1+i\right]\varphi_\text{exp}\right)}.
    \label{eq:exponetial}
\end{equation}
This solution for the vertical momentum flux in the exponential model is shown in \autoref{fig:exponential_structure} for different non-dimensional heights, $\varphi_\text{exp}$. For smaller $\varphi_\text{exp}$, the solution is more localized toward the surface, whereas larger $\varphi_\text{exp}$ produces a more extended spiral. This implies that for $\varphi_\text{exp}\gg1$, the viscosity changes very little, causing the spiral to behave as in the one-layer model. In contrast, $\varphi_\text{exp}\ll 1$ indicate a rapid viscosity decrease from the ground.

Using \autoref{eq:exponetial} the surface angle is 
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \ang{45} + \arg\left[\frac{K_0\left(4\left[1+i\right]\,\varphi_\text{exp}\,\right)}{K_1\left(4\left[1+i\right]\,\varphi_\text{exp}\,\right)}\right],
    \label{eq:ExponetialAngle}
\end{equation} 
where $K_1$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order one. \autoref{eq:ExponetialAngle} is plotted in \autoref{fig:exponential_angle}. As this model only provides scenarios where the viscosity decreases from the ground, all angels are greater then the reference angle of \ang{45} (as predicted by the two layer model). One can observe a similar limit as in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle} when $\varphi_\text{exp}\rightarrow 0$ for $\nu_2=0$, as \autoref{eq:ExponetialAngle} also approaches \ang{90}. The same limit is also true for $\varphi_\text{exp}\to 1$, which gives $\theta\rightarrow\ang{45}$, although this exponential solution converges slightly slower. 

The opposite case when $\varphi_\text{exp}\ll1$ allows for Taylor expansion of $\tau(z)$ about $z=0$ as
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    \tau(z) &\approx \tau_0+ \tau'(0)z+ \tau''(0)\frac{z^2}{2} \\
    &\approx \tau_0 -\underbrace{iU_gz}_\text{linear} + \underbrace{\frac{\tau_0i}{h_\text{Ek0}^2}z^2}_\text{curvature}. \\
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Comparing the curvature term with $\tau_0$ gives a linear region of $\sim h_\text{Ek0}$ when $\varphi\ll1$. However, at $z\sim h_\text{Ek0}$, the momentum flux is approximately zero $\tau\approx0$ according to Ekman theory. Thus the surface momentum flux has to be imaginary to the leading order to fulfil the vanishing upper boundary condition, providing a perpendicular surface angle of \ang{90} when $\varphi_\text{exp}\to 0$.

\subsection{The simplified upper boundary condition model}
There is difficulty solving \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} due to the altitude dependence of the viscosity $\nu(z)$. Analytical solutions therefore exist only for specific viscosity profiles that allow the equation to be transformed into a solvable form. \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is furthermore also difficult to solve numerically, as the upper boundary condition is asymptotic. This will put limits on the viscosity profiles to study, as the viscosity cannot increase with altitude continuously. In the physical world, this makes sense, since we assume that high up in the atmosphere (or deep down in the ocean), the flow becomes laminar and the turbulent viscosity small.

A solution to solve this problem is to introduce a simplified upper boundary condition. For the two-layer model, this is reduced to a 1.5 layer model, where we assume that the upper-level viscosity is negligible ($\nu_2\approx0$), which corresponds to the upper dashed curve in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. This also makes physical sense, since we know that both the atmosphere and the ocean consist of a boundary layer where turbulent mixing takes place. Allowing the viscosity to vary freely in the lower layer reduces the problem to a simplified upper boundary condition model. This will allow viscosity profiles that do not converge at $z\rightarrow\infty$, since the new domain is between $z\in[0,H]$. This modifies the boundary conditions in \autoref{eq:MomentumFluxBoundaryConditions} as 
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:NewBoundaryConditions}
\begin{align}
    \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z}\right|_{z=0} &= -ifU_g,
    \label{eq:NewBoundaryCondition1}
    \\
    \tau(z=H) &= 0.
    \label{eq:NewBoundaryCondition2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $H$ is the height of the boundary layer, and the upper boundary condition was argued from the jump condition in the two-layer model, as seen in \autoref{eq:JumpCondition}.

\subsubsection{Simple viscosity profiles for the SUBC model}
Using the the simplified upper boundary condition, SUBC model, simple viscosity profiles can be studied and evaluated numerically. The easiest, except for the constant viscosity, is a linearly varying viscosity with height as 
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:LinearProfiles}   
 \begin{align}
    \label{eq:LinearIncreasing}
    \nu_\uparrow(z)&=\frac{\nu_\text{max}}{H}z, \\
    \label{eq:LinearDecreasing}
    \nu_\downarrow(z)&=\frac{\nu_\text{max}}{H}\left(H-z\right), 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\nu_\text{max}$ is the maximal viscosity. For both of these profiles a dimensionless vertical coordinate can be defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \tilde z = \frac{z}{H} \implies 
    \begin{cases}
        \nu_\uparrow(z)=\nu_\text{max}\tilde z \\
        \nu_\downarrow(z)=\nu_\text{max}\left(1-\tilde z\right). 
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Using these linear viscosity profiles, together with the new dimensionless vertical coordinate, \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is rewritten for the two profiles as 
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:SUBCLinear}
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:SUBCLinearIncreasing}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d} \tilde z^2}&=i\underbrace{\frac{1}{ \tilde z}}_{\equiv 1/\tilde\nu_\uparrow}\underbrace{\frac{H^2f}{\nu_\text{max}}}_{=2\varphi^2}\tau,\\
    \label{eq:SUBCLinearDecreasing}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d} \tilde z^2}&=i\underbrace{\frac{1}{ 1-\tilde z}}_{\equiv 1/\tilde\nu_\downarrow}\underbrace{\frac{H^2f}{\nu_\text{max}}}_{=2\varphi^2}\tau.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Above the dimensionless thickness $\varphi$ was again introduced following the logic from before.

Another simple profile to evaluate is a parabolic increasing viscosity as 
\begin{equation} 
    \nu_p=\frac{4\nu_\text{max}}{H^2}\left(H-z\right)z.
    \label{eq:ParabolicProfile}
\end{equation} 
Again, using the non-dimensional height coordinate $\tilde z$ and the dimensionless thickness $\varphi$, \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} is rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\tau}{\mathrm{d} \tilde z^2}=i \underbrace{\frac{1}{ 4\tilde z \left[1-\tilde z\right]}}_{\equiv 1/\tilde\nu_p} \underbrace{\frac{H^2f}{\nu_\text{max}}}_{=2\varphi^2}(U-U_g).
    \label{eq:SUBCParabolic}
\end{equation}
Since the height coordinate is normalized for the height $H$, the dimensionless thickness simply becomes the same as the inverse of the normalized Ekman scale height, defined as
\begin{equation}
    \varphi=\frac{H}{h_\text{Ek{(max)}}}=\frac{1}{\tilde h_\text{Ek{(max)}}}.
\end{equation}
