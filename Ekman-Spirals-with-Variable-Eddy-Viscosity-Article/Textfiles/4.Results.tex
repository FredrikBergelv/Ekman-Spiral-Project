\section{Results}

\subsection{The theoretical models}

The result from the theoretical two layer model, \autoref{eq:2layerAngle}, can be seen in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. There one can observe solutions for different viscosity ratios, half of which have an higher upper-level viscosity and the other half a lower one. These are plotted together with a \ang{45} reference point to highlight the difference from the classical Ekman solution. One can observe a convergence towards the classical Ekman value when $\varphi\rightarrow\infty$ for all the viscosity ratios. The zero limit is, however, dependent on the viscosity ratio, where finite ratios approach \ang{45}. However, when $\tfrac{\nu_2}{\nu_1}\rightarrow0$ and $\varphi\rightarrow 0$ one can observe that $\theta\rightarrow\ang{90}$. Respectively, one can observe that when $\tfrac{\nu_2}{\nu_1}\rightarrow\infty$ and $\varphi\rightarrow0$, $\theta\rightarrow\ang{0}$. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.37\textwidth]{Figures/2layer_angle.png}
    \caption{The surface angle as a function of the dimensionless layer thickness $\varphi = H/h_\text{Ek1}$ for the two-layer viscosity model. Different coloured curves correspond to different layer viscosity ratios. The classical Ekman angle of \ang{45} is shown as a reference.}
    \label{fig:2layer_angle}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:exponential_angle} showcases the result from the exponentially decaying viscosity as in \autoref{eq:ExponetialAngle}. There one can observe a similar limit as in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle} when $\varphi\rightarrow 0$, as \autoref{eq:ExponetialAngle} also approaches \ang{90}. The same limit is also true for $\varphi\rightarrow\infty$, which gives $\theta\rightarrow\ang{45}$, although this exponential solution converges more slowly than the two-layer solution. However, the shape is much more uniform, with an exponential-like shape. However, all angels are here greater then the reference angle of \ang{45}. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.37\textwidth]{Figures/exponential_angle.png}
    \caption{Surface angle for the exponentially decaying viscosity model as a function of the dimensionless parameter $\varphi=H/h_\text{Ek}$. The solution assumed a purely zonal geostrophic flow.}
    \label{fig:exponential_angle}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The SUBC model}
The result from the numerical SUBC model can be viewed in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle}. There one can observe similarities between these models, the two-layer model, and the exponentially decaying model. They all have a lower boundary of \ang{90} (except for the decreasing two-layer model, where the lower boundary was \ang{0}). This strengthens the results from these numerical models, as the theoretical ones behave in a similar manner.

The result from the linearly decreasing SUBC model is shown in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}, where one can observe that the values go smoothly from $\ang{90}\rightarrow\ang{45}$ as $\varphi$ increases. This plot resembles the exponential decaying model seen in \autoref{fig:exponential_angle} and also the 1.5 model as seen in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. 


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.37\textwidth]{Figures/numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle.png}
    \caption{Numerically computed surface angles for decreasing viscosity profiles in the SUBC model. The angle is dependent on the dimensionless parameter $\varphi=H/h_\text{Ek}$. The solution assumed a purely zonal geostrophic flow.}
    \label{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}
\end{figure}

However, for the linearly increasing viscosity a different result can be seen in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle}. The upper boundary is instead located at \ang{0}, and the curve crosses \ang{45}. One can observe different shapes for the different background viscosities, $\epsilon$, although they all exhibit the same behaviour: an initial large angle decrease, a minimum, and then a slow increase. One can also observe that the lines show a sharper decrease and flatter behaviour for the smaller background viscosities.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.37\textwidth]{Figures/numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle.png}
    \caption{Numerically computed surface angles for linearly increasing. The angle is dependent on the dimensionless parameter $\varphi=H/h_\text{Ek}$ and the background viscosity $\epsilon$. The solution assumed a purely zonal geostrophic flow.}
    \label{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle}
\end{figure}

The SUBC model with a parabolic viscosity profile gave \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle}. There one can observe very similar behaviour to the increasing viscosity profile in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle}. However, the two figures differ slightly in that the parabolic curves cross the \ang{45} later than the linearly increasing curves. Furthermore, the minimum is less pronounced compared to the linear increasing plot.
