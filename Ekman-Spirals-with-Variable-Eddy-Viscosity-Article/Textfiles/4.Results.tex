\subsection{The vertical structure of the momentum flux}

The vertical profiles from the numerical SUBC model can be viewed in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_structure}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_structure}. One can view this together with the normalized Ekman height for the different layer. An important note is that when $\varphi<1$ then the Ekman heights become larger than the limit $H$. This is the same as stating that the entire spiral occurs really close to the surface. 

Since the surface wind-turning angle is calculated by comparing the initial change of the viscosity, both the linear increasing and the parabolic models are dependent on the background viscosity $\epsilon$. The effect of varying $\epsilon$ can be observed in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_structure}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_structure}.

\subsection{The surface angles}

\autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle} show the surface angles from the numerical SUBC models. Similarities between these models, the increasing two-layer model, and the exponentially decaying model can be observed. They all have a lower boundary of \ang{90} and an upper boundary of \ang{45}. The reason for the similarly between the exponential model and the increasing two layer model, is that the SUBC model is based on the increasing two layer model. 

Thus, in the limit when $\varphi\to 0$ the Ekman height becomes infinitely larger then the interface $H$, providing a 1.5 layer like solution which has the the limit of \ang{90}. When $\varphi\to \infty$ the maximum value of the profile decreases, providing a more constant like solution, with a smaller viscosity change with altitude. Thus the solution approaches the classical Ekman solution, with an angle of \ang{45}. 

The result from the linearly decreasing SUBC model is shown in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}, where the values smoothly transition from $\ang{90}\rightarrow\ang{45}$ as $\varphi$ increases. This plot resembles the exponential decaying model seen in \autoref{fig:exponential_angle} and  the 1.5 model as seen in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. Again, all angles are larger then the reference angle of \ang{45}. The close resemblance to the exponential and 1.5 layer solutions makes sense since this solution is also a strictly decreasing solution. 

\begin{figure*}[hbt!]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.42\textwidth]{Figures/numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle.png}
    \caption{Numerically computed surface angles for decreasing viscosity profiles in the SUBC model. The angle is dependent on the dimensionless parameter $\varphi=H/h_\text{Ek}$. The solution assumed a purely zonal geostrophic flow.}
    \label{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}
\end{figure*}

For the linearly increasing viscosity a different result can be seen in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle}. The upper boundary is again located at \ang{45}, but at a very large non-dimensional height ratio. For smaller background viscosities, $\epsilon$, this limit occurs at many magnitudes higher then in the previous models. Since the eigenvalue is proportional to the inverse o the viscosity, smaller background viscosities yields way larger eigenvalues as seen in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_structure}. Thus, this limit of \ang{45} occurs at way higher non-dimensional heights. 

The curve also the obtains values smaller then \ang{45} outside of the limits discussed above. Since the limits are the extreme cases, this interior is the more interesting part of the result. The surface wind-turning angle is smaller then \ang{45} since a larger viscosity ata a higher altitude means that the turning of the wind is smaller near the surface and greater aloft. This forces the surface angles to become less then \ang{45} near the ground. 

Another interesting observation is that, away from the limits, she surface wind-turning angle is more dependent on the background viscosity, $\epsilon$, rather then the non-dimensional height, especially for the lower $\epsilon$. This occurs since the eigenvalue is the inverse of the viscosity and that the initial increase from the ground to the domain is the most crucial one for the surface angle. Thus, if the background viscosity is much smaller then the maximal viscosity then smaller $\epsilon$ is obtained and the initial eigenvalue jump from the surface becomes greater. In the theoretical limit when $\epsilon\to0$, the initial eigenvalue jump would be infinite, and the surface angle would be zero.

The SUBC model with a parabolic viscosity profile gave \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle}. Very similar behaviour to the increasing viscosity profile in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle} can be observed. Since the viscosity jump from the surface is what determines the surface angle, the parabolic decrees aloft has no dramatic effect on the surface angle. The two models differ slightly in that the parabolic curves cross the \ang{45} later than the linearly increasing curves.

\subsubsection{The Ekman transport}
Computing the transport as by the surface vertical momentum flux as \autoref{eq:Transport} yielded exactly the same shapes as seen in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle}, but centerd around \ang{135} rather then \ang{45}. The angle differences between the surface angles and the transport angle was thus always \ang{90}, just like in the classical Ekman theory.
