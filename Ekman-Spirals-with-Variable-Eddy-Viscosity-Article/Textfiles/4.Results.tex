\section{Result}
\subsection{The theoretical surface angles}

\subsubsection{The vertical structure of the momentum flux}
The vertical profiles from the numerical SUBC model can be viewed in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_structure}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_structure} together with the normalized Ekman height for the different layers. An important note is that when $\varphi<1$ then the Ekman heights become larger than the limit $H$, and the spiral therefore extends over a large height. In contrast, if $\varphi\gg1$, then the Ekman height is much smaller than the layer depth, $h_\text{Ek}\ll H$, such that the spiral is closer ot the surface as $z\ll H$. Another important observation in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_structure}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_structure} is that for $ \varphi < 1 $, almost linear profiles are observed. This supports the claim above that when $ \varphi \ll 1 $, then $ \tau(z) $ in \autoref{eq:MomentumFlux} behaves as a linear function. 

Since the surface wind angle is calculated by comparing the initial change of the viscosity, both the linear increasing and the parabolic models are dependent on the background viscosity $\epsilon$. The effect of varying $\epsilon$ can be observed in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_structure}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_structure}. However for the linear decreasing model, no major effect could be observed by varying the background viscosity. Thus, a background viscosity of \SI{1e-6}{} was used for this specific model.

\subsubsection{The surface angles}

\autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle} show the surface angles from the numerical SUBC models. Similarities between these models, the decreasing two-layer model, and the exponentially decaying model can be observed. They all have a lower limit of \ang{90} and an upper limit of \ang{45}. The reason for the similarly between the exponential model and the increasing two layer model, is that the SUBC model is based on the decreasing two layer model, as $\nu=0$ above the height $H$. Again the linear behaviour of $\tau(z)$ when $\varphi\ll1$ yields a surface angle of \ang{90}, while the more constant viscosity when $\varphi\gg1$ yields a surface angle of \ang{45}. Thus, in the limit when $\varphi\to 0$ the Ekman height becomes infinitely larger then  $H$ providing a 1.5 layer like solution which has the the limit of \ang{90} and when $\varphi\to \infty$ the maximum value of the profile decreases, providing a smaller change with altitude, and a more constant like viscosity with a classical angle of \ang{45}. 

The result from the linearly decreasing SUBC model is shown in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}, where the values smoothly transition from $\ang{90}\rightarrow\ang{45}$ as $\varphi$ increases. This plot resembles the exponential decaying model seen in \autoref{fig:exponential_angle} and  the 1.5 model as seen in \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}. Again, all angles are larger then the reference angle of \ang{45}. The close resemblance to the exponential and 1.5 layer solutions makes sense since this solution is also a strictly decreasing solution. 

\begin{figure*}[hbt!]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.42\textwidth]{Figures/numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle.png}
    \caption{Numerically computed surface angles for decreasing viscosity profiles in the SUBC model. The angles are dependent on the dimensionless parameter $\varphi=H/h_\text{Ek}$. }
    \label{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}
\end{figure*}

For the linearly increasing viscosity in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle} a different result can be seen. The upper limit is again located at \ang{45}, but at very large non-dimensional heights. For smaller background viscosities the limit occurs at many magnitudes higher then for the previous models due to a much larger $1/{\tilde\nu_\uparrow}$-factors.  

The curve obtains values smaller then \ang{45} outside of the limits discussed above. Since the lower limit is an inherent property of the SUBC model and the upper limit is well understood, makes the interior more interesting. The surface wind angle is smaller then \ang{45} since a larger viscosity at a higher altitude means that the rotation of the wind is smaller near the surface and greater aloft. This forces the surface angles to become less then \ang{45}. This can be compared to the two-layer model with a small lower viscosity $\nu_1$ and small interface height $H$. Observing \autoref{fig:2layer_angle}, one can see that this scenario also yielded angles smaller than \ang{45}, like in this figure.

Another interesting observation is that, away from the limits, she surface wind angle is more sensitive to the background viscosity than to the non-dimensional height. If the background viscosity is much smaller then the maximal viscosity then a smaller $\epsilon$ gives a huge $1/{\tilde\nu_\uparrow}$ jump from the surface. In the theoretical limit when $\epsilon\to0$, the initial jump would be infinite, and the surface angle would be zero.

The SUBC model with a parabolic viscosity profile gave \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle}. Very similar behaviour to the increasing viscosity profile in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_i_angle} can be observed. Since the viscosity jump from the surface is what determines the surface angle, the parabolic decrees aloft has no dramatic effect on the surface angle. The two models differ slightly in that the parabolic curves cross the \ang{45} later than the linearly increasing curves.

\subsubsection{The Ekman transport}
Computing the transport with the surface vertical momentum flux as \autoref{eq:Transport} yielded exactly the same shapes as seen in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_angle}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_angle}, but centred around \ang{135} rather then \ang{45}, thus a difference of \ang{90} like in the classical Ekman theory. 

The transport values in \autoref{fig:numerical_SUBC_linear_d_transport}--\ref{fig:numerical_SUBC_parabolic_transport} show similar behaviour with maximum zonal transport when $\varphi\to0$ and maximal meridional transport between $\varphi\in(0,1)$.

\subsection{The ERA5 surface angles}
\SI{24535}{} data points were fit for analysis from the ERA5 dataset. These surface wind angles, due to the presence of a jet stream for flat land points in the northern midlatitudes, are shown in \autoref{fig:ERA5_angles}. The geographical spread of the data points can be observed in \autoref{fig:ERA5_angle_map}, while \autoref{fig:ERA5_angle_histogram} shows the probability distribution. \autoref{fig:ERA5_angle_histogram} shows a wide range of surface angles, but with a clear peak between \ang{0}--\ang{60}. Comparing this peak with the reference value of \ang{45} shows that a clear majority of the surface angles are smaller than the reference, although a minority can be observed to be greater than the reference angle. The larger spread between -\ang{180} to \ang{180} may arise from other mereological processes, such as the presence of a semi-permanent high- and low-pressure system, causing surface winds. Due to the smoothness of these values, these can be dismissed as outliers and the focus can lie on the peak between \ang{0}--\ang{60}.
