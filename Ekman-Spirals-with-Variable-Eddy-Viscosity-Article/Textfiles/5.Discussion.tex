\section{Discussion}

From both analytical and numerical models, angles between \ang{0} to \ang{90} could be observed. Generally, surface wind angles above the classical value of \ang{45} could be observed with strictly decreasing viscosities with altitude. The only case where this did not occur was for a small $\varphi$-region in the two-layer model, with a lowest value of \ang{43.4}. The reasoning behind decreasing viscosities with altitude providing surface angles over \ang{45} value can be attributed to decreasing viscosities forcing rotation to occur at a lower height. As the viscosity is initially large but decreases with height, the spiral is forced to turn more at the lower altitudes. This has to occur in order to satisfy the geostrophic limit above. Thus, the surface angle becomes larger than the classical value for the decreasing viscosities with altitude.

For the increasing viscosities with altitude, the opposite could be observed with smaller surface wind angles than the classical value. For the two-layer model, only a small region had angles greater than the classical value. However, the largest value was only measured at \ang{46.6}, which is close to the classical limit. The same logic follows to explain why this surface angle became smaller, as the explanation for why the surface angles became larger for the decreasing viscosities with height. If the viscosity increases with height, then the spiral has to turn slower near the surface in order to turn enough to reach the geostrophic limit aloft. And since the viscosity increases with altitude, more rotation can occur there, as it is the shear due to the viscosity which forces this rotation. 

The same reasoning can be used to explain the limits for the surface angles when the dimensionless layer thickness goes to infinity or zero. Since the dimensionless layer thickness is the ratio between the characteristic height and the Ekman height scale, it follows that when the ratio goes towards infinity, a constant viscosity is obtained, and the angles go to the classical value of \ang{45}. Furthermore, when the ratio between the characteristic height and the Ekman height scale goes towards zero, one would obtain the extremes of the logic presented in the paragraphs above. Since the Coriolis force is perpendicular to the flow, a \ang{90} surface angle will be obtained if the viscosity decreases with altitude, since the entire rotation has to occur at the surface. On the other hand, if the viscosity increases with height, then no part of the rotation can occur at the surface, and a parallel angle of \ang{0} will be obtained at the surface. This could be explained more mathematically as when $\varphi \ll 1$ the solution behaved approximately as a linear function, causing a shift in the phase of the surface stress. Because $U(z)\approx \tau_0 z/\nu(0)$ close to the surface, this phase directly determines the surface wind angle, approaching \ang{90} for decreasing viscosities and \ang{0} for increasing viscosities.


Comparing the results from the theoretical models with the ERA5 data, a clear connection can be observed. The ERA5 data showed a clear majority of angles between \ang{0} and \ang{45}. As the atmosphere consists of a turbulent boundary layer viscosity generally increases with height, which connects the theoretical models to the observed data. However, a small portion of the data that had angles above \ang{45}, although a boundary layer must be present. This can be attributed to the boundary layer having a small region where the viscosity decreases with height, or a strictly decreasing one producing slightly higher angels like in the two-layer model, which would allow surface angles above \ang{45}. Although one should not forget that the ERA5 data are reanalysed data, and thus not pure observations, which also could explain this portion of the data. However, the bulk of the data is still in agreement with the theoretical models, supporting the findings in this article.

Comparing these results with previous works shows a general consistency. The general rule that strictly increasing viscosities with height produce surface angles below \ang{45} and strictly decreasing viscosities with height produce surface angles above \ang{45} can be compared to the previously mentioned earlier work. \citet{ionescu-kruse2021} showed that by analysing two smoothly increasing polynomial viscosity profiles, only one of the two produced angles below \ang{45}, with a quadratic profile producing angles above \ang{45}. This was, however, corrected by \citet{guan2023}, who showed that the quadratic profile produced angles below \ang{45}, agreeing with the findings in this article. The piecewise constant two-layer models by \citet{dritschel2020} agreed with the findings here, that increasing viscosity yielded angles below \ang{45} and vice versa. The perturbative studies by \citet{bressan2019} and \citet{constantin2021} also support the findings in this article. \citet{constantin2021} highlighted that even strictly decreasing viscosities could yield angles below \ang{45} and vice versa, like the two-layer model in this study. However, it seems like this can only occur for non-smoothly varying viscosities, as all the other models in this article and the previous works show that smoothly increasing viscosities yield angles below \ang{45} and smoothly decreasing viscosities yield angles above \ang{45}.
