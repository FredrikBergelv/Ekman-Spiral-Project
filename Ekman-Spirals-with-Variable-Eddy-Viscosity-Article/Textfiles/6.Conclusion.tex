\section{Conclusion}
I%n this article, a varying eddy viscosity with altitude was studied under the Ekman equation. This was done both theoretically through a dimensionless height ratio, and analytically and numerically, and compared to reanalysed data from ERA5. Analytical solutions for the Ekman spiral were derived for a two-layer model and an exponentially decaying viscosity profile. 
The two-layer model showed that strictly increasing viscosities with height mostly produced surface angles below \ang{45}, while strictly decreasing viscosities mostly produced surface angles above \ang{45}. The exponentially decaying model showed surface angles above \ang{45}. The numerical model used a simplified upper boundary condition, which allowed for any viscosity profile to be studied. The linearly decreasing model gave surface angles above \ang{45}, while the linearly increasing model and the parabolic model produced surface angles below, neglecting extremes caused by the new boundary condition. This showcased that the viscosity jump from the surface is what generally determines the surface angle, as increasing viscosities with height force the turning of the wind to occur aloft, while decreasing viscosities with height force the turning of the wind to occur near the surface. This was even true for the parabolic model, which had a decreasing viscosity aloft, giving angles below \ang{45}. The analytical two-layer model could produce surface angles outside this rule, but only in a small region with lower magnitude angles. 

From ERA5 data a clear majority of angles between \ang{0} and \ang{45} could be observed, supporting the atmosphere having an increasing viscosity near the surface, which agrees with the theory of the mixing length hypothesis and the boundary layer. This article has demonstrated how a combination of analytical, numerical, and reanalysed data can be used together to obtain a deeper understanding of the Ekman spiral and the effect of varying eddy viscosities with height. The results from this article can thus be used to further understand the Ekman spiral and thus get a deeper knowledge of the boundary layer in the atmosphere and ocean.
