\section{Introduction}
The Ekman spiral is an important consequence of the Coriolis force in meteorology and oceanography. Due to the interaction between shear forces in the medium the wind high up in the atmosphere, or wind over the ocean, will not give rise to a flow in the same direction as at the surface. The adjustment of the flow from the surface spirals and is therefore properly named the Ekman spiral after its discoverer \citet{ekman1905}. According to \citet{ekman1905} the surface wind is at an angle from the wind aloft by \ang{45} \citep{ekman1905}. The net flux of the wind is perpendicular to the wind aloft which is known as the Ekman transport and is crucial concept in meteorology and oceanography.

However, a surface wind-turning of \ang{45} assumes a constant viscosity with height, which is unrealistic for a turbulent boundary layer. The boundary layer is around \SI{1}{}--\SI{2}{\kilo\meter} thick and is characterised by a presence of turbulence \citep{wallace2006}. The turbulence primarily arises from solar heating, causing instability and turnover, but also from mechanical turbulence from mean wind decreasing closer to the ground due to frictional shear forces. Although turbulence is non-linear, statistical analysis can provide useful first-order approximations, such as the downward flux being negatively and linearly proportional to the local gradient. Using this proportionality constant one can define the eddy viscosity $\nu$ for the boundary layer.

According to the mixing length hypothesis, the eddy viscosity should grow with the distance from the surface as the characteristic scales of the eddies grow. Dimensional analysis of the boundary layer describes a logarithmic relation between the wind speed and height  \citep{wallace2006}. This showcases that the eddies, and thus the eddy viscosity, grow with height in the boundary layer. However, after a certain altitude, the turbulent boundary layer ceases to exist, and laminar flow takes over, which rapidly decreases the eddy viscosity. Thus, assuming initial increase of the eddy viscosity followed by decrees at higher altitude is reasonable. Following this logic, the eddy viscosity should in reality vary with altitude, and thus changing the surface wind-turning of \ang{45}. 

Analytical models have investigated the effect of depth-dependent eddy viscosities for the Ekman spiral. \citet{grisogono1995} used a Wentzel--Kramers--Brillouin (WKB) approximation, like in quantum mechanics, for the classical Ekman solution, showcasing that smoothly varying viscosity profiles allow for approximate analytical solutions for the entire Ekman height. \citet{bressan2019} used a direct perturbative method on the viscosity in order to obtain a first-order equation for predicting the surface angle of a constant perturbed viscosity. This was later refined by \citet{constantin2021} to show that the surface angle is a product of the classical angle and a weighted vertical integral of the perturbation. Some exact analytical solutions have also been derived, including a two-layer model by \citet{dritschel2020, guan2023}, an exponential decaying profile by \citet{miles1994} and monotonically increasing polynomial profiles by \citet{ionescu-kruse2021,guan2023}.

Further insight into the effect of the variable eddy viscosity can be obtained by comparing analytical, numerical solutions and observational data. In this article, a varying eddy viscosity with altitude will be studied under the Ekman equation. This will be done analytically and numerically, and will be compared to reanalysed data. This will give a deeper understanding of the Ekman spiral and provide insight when a surface angle different from \ang{45} can be observed.
